# Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information (Wineburg and McGrew, Teachers College Record 2019)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912101102
- PDF: pdf/lateral-reading-wineburg-2019.pdf
- Status: fetched in full (in-press version dated July 28, 2018, from Stanford Digital Repository)
- Why it matters for source trust: shows the behaviour that works for judging an unknown site is to leave it and check what other sources say, not to inspect the site itself.

## What it says
Ten historians, ten professional fact checkers and twenty-five Stanford undergraduates evaluated live websites while thinking aloud. Fact checkers were faster and more accurate on every task. Historians and students read vertically: they stayed on the page and judged it from its logo, domain, references and tone. Fact checkers read laterally: they left the page within seconds, opened new tabs and searched for what others said about the organisation. On the bullying task 60% of students judged the lobby group more reliable than the paediatric academy. Fact checkers also showed click restraint, scanning a results page before choosing a link. The authors argue that expertise in a subject does not transfer to judging a site, and that lateral reading is teachable.

## Method
1. Recruit 45 participants: 10 PhD historians, 10 fact checkers from well-regarded news and political fact-checking organisations, 25 undergraduates paid a $25 gift card (Participants).
2. Run six think-aloud tasks in about 45 minutes on a live internet connection; three are reported (Tasks).
3. Task 1: compare the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American College of Pediatricians on bullying, 5 minutes per site. Task 2: judge minimumwage.com (run by the Employment Policies Institute, a Berman and Company front), 5 minutes plus 3 prompted minutes. Task 3: Vergara v. California article.
4. Score reliability judgements on a 0 to 2 rubric (Task 1) and conclusions out of 4 (Task 2); two raters, 92% agreement on 22% of the corpus (Coding).
5. Time how long each participant stayed on the landing page and how many sites they opened; compare groups with Kruskal-Wallis tests.

## Claims we can test against
1. Judging a source from its own page (design, domain, references) produces wrong answers even for PhD experts.
2. Leaving the page and reading what third parties say yields the right answer in under a minute.
3. Time on the target page is inversely related to accuracy.
4. A trust system should weight external descriptions of a source over its self-presentation.
